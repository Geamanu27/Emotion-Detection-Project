\subsection{First Sub Question:}
\textbf{How does the ethnicity of a person affect the performance and fairness of mood detection models?}

\subsubsection*{Introduction to Algorithmic Bias}
Computer vision models are not inherently neutral; they reflect the data they are trained on. In the context of facial emotion recognition (FER), the ethnicity of a subject significantly impacts model performance. If a dataset is unbalanced (containing mostly images of one demographic group), the model will "overfit" to the facial features, skin tones, and lighting conditions typical of that group, while failing to generalize to others. This phenomenon is known as Algorithmic Bias.

\subsubsection*{The Dataset Problem: FER2013 vs. Reality}
The model developed in this project was initially trained on the FER2013 dataset. Research indicates that FER2013, like many early academic datasets, is heavily skewed towards White/Western faces. This can lead to a number of problems: 
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Feature Overfitting:} The MobileNet model learns to detect emotions based on specific landmarks (e.g., the curve of a mouth, the crinkle of an eye). If the training data lacks diversity, the model may treat features common to specific ethnicities (such as different eye shapes or brow structures) as indicators of specific emotions rather than neutral physical traits.

\item \textbf{Performance Gap:} Consequently, when the model is tested on non-Western faces (e.g., Asian or Black individuals), the accuracy drops. The "recall" score (The ability to find all examples of an emotion) is significantly lower for minority groups because the model simply hasn't seen enough examples to recognize their expressions confidently.
\end{enumerate}
\newpage
\subsubsection*{The "Angry Black Man" Paradox}
One of the most documented and harmful failures in facial recognition fairness is the "Angry Black Man" paradox. This occurs when a model disproportionately misclassifies "Neutral" or "Serious" expressions of Black men as "Angry."

\textbullet \textbf{Cause:} The model learns to associate darker skin tones or specific natural resting face structures with the label "Angry" because the few examples of Black individuals in the training set might have been biased towards negative emotions, or simply because the model confuses physical features with emotional signals.

\textbullet \textbf{Impact:} In a real-world deployment (e.g., an automated interview screener or a security system), this bias could lead to unfair penalization or profiling of Black individuals, flagging them as aggressive when they are simply neutral. This violates the fairness principle outlined in the EU AI Act \cite{aiact}.

\subsubsection*{The "Polite Smile" Paradox}
A significant paradox affects Asian demographics, often rooted in Cultural Display Rules rather than just physical features. This paradox occurs when models incorrectly classify expressions of embarrassment, anxiety, or politeness as "Happy."

\textbullet \textbf{Cause:} Western-centric datasets like FER2013 almost exclusively annotate the geometric feature of an upturned mouth as "Happiness." However, in many East Asian cultures (e.g., Japan, Thailand), smiling is frequently used as a social tool to mask negative emotions like embarrassment or confusion. Because the model lacks this cultural context, it sees the geometry of a smile and ignores other subtle cues (like the eyes) that might indicate distress.

\textbullet \textbf{Impact:} This bias is critical in healthcare or wellbeing applications. For example, an AI therapist or mental health monitoring app might incorrectly classify a distressed Asian user as "Happy" or "Doing well" simply because they are smiling out of polite discomfort. This leads to a false positive for well-being, potentially denying the user necessary help.

\subsubsection*{Mitigation Strategies and AffectNet}
To address these ethnic disparities, simply training for more epochs is insufficient. The underlying data must be corrected:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Dataset Balancing:} Moving from FER2013 to larger, like AffectNet provides a more balanced representation of global demographics, reducing the performance gap.

\item \textbf{Bias Auditing:} Running a "Bias Audit" (testing the model on a balanced dataset) is crucial to identifying and quantifying these risks before deployment.
\end{enumerate}
\subsubsection*{Conclusion}
Ethnicity plays a critical role in the reliability of mood detection models. Without intentional intervention, models trained on standard public datasets will exhibit significant bias, performing accurately on White faces while misinterpreting or under-performing on minority groups. Ensuring fairness requires a shift from model-centric optimization to data-centric curation, ensuring all ethnicities are represented equally in the training phase.

\subsection{Second Sub Question:}
\textbf{How does reat-time camera input compare to static images or pre-recorded videos in terms of accuracy and usability?}\\\\
The performance of emotion detection systems differs noticeably on static images, pre-recorded videos and real-time camera input. During the development and testing of the desktop application the differences were clearly observed in both accuracy and usability when using real-time webcam input compared to controlled data, such as pre-recorded videos and images.\\
When using static images, the emotion detection model achieved the highest accuracy. Static images are usually well-aligned, centered and captured under controlled light conditions. In most of the public datasets, such as FER2013 and AffectNet, facial expressions are exaggerated and clearly labeled, which makes the job for the model much easier. Because of the high-quality frame, there is no motion blur or occlusion and the predictions tend to be more stable.\\
Pre-recoreded videos tend to be the middle ground between static images and real-time imput. Videos provide multiple frames of the same expression, allowing the model to use more complex algorithms, like majority voting. However, pre-recorded videos are still most of the time captured in controlled environments, often showing clear facial expressions. As a result, while performance is still lower than a static images due to motion blur and lighting inconsistencies, accuracy tends to remain relatively high and consistent.\\
Real-time camera input introduces several challenges that affect accuracy. In real life, facial expressions are often subtle, incomplete or mixed. While testing the desktop application, it was observed thta the model struggled with detecting mild frustration or subtle anger. These emotions were sometimes misclassified as NEUTRAL rather than NEGATIVE. This is a clear example of why emotion training datasets often do not reflect real life scenarios, because in the datasets, frustration is often showed by wide eyes or exaggerated frowns, but in real life, frustration does not always involve strong facial cues.\\
Another interesting observation is that this issue occured far less for the POSITIVE class. Positive emotions such as happiness tend to produce clearer and more distinctive facial features, such as smiles or raised cheeks, which are easier to detect in real-time conditions. This resulted in stable and accurate detection for the POSITIVE class.\\
From an usability perspective, real-time camera input is much more challenging but also more realistic. While static images and pre-recorded videos are useful for training and benchmarking, they do not reflect the real world situations. Real-time input captures the face in different light conditions, has to take into account head movements, partial occlusions and spontaneous expressions. All of these factors reduce the classification accuracy but increse external validity. In other words, real-time performance is a more meaningful indicator of how the system is going to perform in practical applications.\\
\subsubsection*{Conclusion}
Overall, static images provide the highest accuracy but the lowest realism, pre-recorded videos offer a balance between accuracy and temporal context, and real-time camera input presents the lowest accuracy due to subtle and natural expressions but the highest usability in real-world scenarios. The observed misclassification of subtle negative emotions as neutral highlights an important limitation of emotion recognition models trained on staged datasets and reinforces the decision to use broader emotion categories in the final application.

\subsection{Third Sub Question:}
\textbf{What ethical considerations need to be taken into account when developing and applying facial emotion recognition systems?}


\subsubsection{Privacy and Data Protection}
Facial data constitutes sensitive biometric information. To minimize risk, the project follows data minimization and purpose limitation, as required by the GDPR \cite{gdpr}. 
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Local processing:} All inference is performed on-device; no raw images or identifiers are transmitted.
  \item \textbf{No permanent storage:} By default, images/frames are processed in memory only. Any diagnostic logging excludes personal data.
  \item \textbf{Data deletion:} Temporary buffers are cleared immediately after inference.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{User Consent and Transparency}
The application implements explicit, informed consent in compliance with Article 6(1)(a) of the GDPR \cite{gdpr}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Opt-in:} Camera access and emotion inference are disabled until the user enables them.
  \item \textbf{Just-in-time notices:} A short notice explains what is processed, for what purpose, and for how long.
  \item \textbf{Controls:} Clear on/off toggle, session indicator (e.g., icon/LED), and a link to the privacy summary.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Data Storage and Security}
If short-term storage is required for evaluation, the design follows the principles of integrity and confidentiality from Article 5 of the GDPR \cite{gdpr} and ISO-style information security best practices.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scope:} Store only what is strictly necessary (e.g., aggregated metrics).
  \item \textbf{Protection:} Encrypt at rest; restrict access to authorized personnel.
  \item \textbf{Retention:} Define retention windows and automatic deletion schedules.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{AI Bias and Fairness}
The model may inherit bias from training data, which is addressed in line with the fairness principles of the EU AI Act \cite{aiact}. Mitigations include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Inspecting class distribution and augmenting underrepresented classes.
  \item Reporting class-wise metrics and confusion matrices.
  \item Avoiding high-stakes use; keeping predictions informational, not determinative.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Ethical Use and Context}
The main goal of this project is to use emotion detection in a positive and helpful way, such as reminding users to take breaks or encouraging them when they show positive emotions. The system is not made for surveillance, profiling, or influencing people’s behaviour. All demonstrations and tests clearly explain the purpose of the system to anyone involved. Users have full control over when the application is active and can pause or stop it at any time. Any future version of the app will show a clear indicator when the camera is on and will include a short, easy-to-understand privacy explanation. By setting clear limits and responsible uses, the project aims to keep emotion detection ethical and focused on helping people, consistent with the IEEE’s principles of human wellbeing and accountability \cite{ieee}.

\subsubsection{Regulatory Alignment}
The project aligns with key European data-protection and AI-ethics frameworks, including the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) \cite{gdpr} and the upcoming EU AI Act \cite{aiact}. Under GDPR, it respects all major principles: lawfulness, fairness, transparency, purpose limitation, data minimisation, and integrity/confidentiality. The design treats all facial analysis as sensitive processing and ensures that it stays within a low-risk category by design—local processing, explicit user consent, and transparent operation. In terms of governance, documentation is maintained for the model’s purpose, assumptions, and limitations to promote accountability. The project also draws on the IEEE Ethically Aligned Design framework \cite{ieee}, focusing on human agency, accountability, and the promotion of wellbeing. Together, these measures ensure that the prototype not only complies with current legal requirements but also anticipates future standards for trustworthy and responsible AI systems.

\subsection{Fourth Sub Question:}
\textbf{How does the model's accuracy in mood detection vary across different emotion categories?}\\
To answer this question, three iterations of the model were evaluated:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Model A:} 7 Classes (All emotions), trained for 25 Epochs.
    \item \textbf{Model B:} 7 Classes (All emotions), trained for 35 Epochs.
    \item \textbf{Model C:} 3 Classes (Positive, Negative, Neutral), trained for 35 Epochs.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The performance of each model was analysed using the per-class F1 scores and confusion matrices. These reveal how reliably the system can distinguish between specific emotions and which categories remain challenging for the MobileNet architecture trained on FER2013.

\subsubsection*{Model A: 7 Classes, 25 Epochs}
Model A shows a clear imbalance in performance across emotion categories. Emotions with highly expressive and distinct facial cues perform best, while subtle or ambiguous categories remain problematic.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{images/MobileNet_25e/mobilenet_accuracy_chart_25e.png}
    \caption{Model A — F1-score per class (7 classes, 25 epochs)}
\end{figure}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Highest-performing emotions:} \emph{happy} (F1 $\approx$ 0.87) and \emph{surprise} (F1 $\approx$ 0.76). These emotions have strong visual signals and are well-represented in FER2013.
    \item \textbf{Moderate performance:} \emph{angry} (F1 $\approx$ 0.59), \emph{neutral} (F1 $\approx$ 0.61), \emph{disgust} (F1 $\approx$ 0.57), and \emph{sad} (F1 $\approx$ 0.55). These emotions often overlap visually, leading to frequent misclassifications.
    \item \textbf{Lowest-performing emotion:} \emph{fear} (F1 $\approx$ 0.42), which is heavily confused with \emph{sad}, \emph{surprise}, and \emph{angry}. This aligns with the literature, as fear expressions are subtle and inconsistently depicted in FER2013.
\end{itemize}
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{images/MobileNet_25e/mobilenet_confusion_matrix_25e.png}
    \caption{Model A — Confusion Matrix (7 classes, 25 epochs)}
\end{figure}
The confusion matrix confirms broad dispersion among negative emotions, suggesting the model struggles to differentiate fine-grained emotional cues under limited training duration.


\newpage
\subsubsection*{Model B: 7 Classes, 35 Epochs}
Increasing the training time to 35 epochs improves overall stability and class separation.
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{images/MobileNet_35e/mobilenet_accuracy_chart_35e.png}
    \caption{Model B — F1-score per class (7 classes, 35 epochs)}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Improvements:} Most emotion categories show higher F1 scores compared to Model A. Notably, \emph{happy} remains the strongest class (F1 $\approx$ 0.89), and \emph{surprise} increases to roughly 0.79.
    \item \textbf{Negative emotion patterns:} \emph{angry}, \emph{disgust}, \emph{sad}, and \emph{neutral} all experience modest improvements, but the confusion matrix still reveals strong overlap among these categories.
    \item \textbf{Fear remains challenging:} Even with extended training, \emph{fear} continues to have the lowest performance (F1 $\approx$ 0.46), confirming its status as the least distinguishable category.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Emotions with clear, distinct facial features (e.g., \emph{happy}, \emph{surprise}) consistently achieve the highest accuracy.
    \item Subtle, low-intensity, or culturally variable emotions (\emph{fear}, \emph{disgust}, \emph{sad}) remain challenging regardless of training duration.
    \item Increasing epochs improves performance but does not fully resolve dataset-driven limitations.
    \item Reducing the classification task to broader emotion groups significantly increases accuracy, especially for Positive and Negative categories.

In summary, the model’s accuracy varies substantially across emotion types, with high performance on expressive emotions and weaker performance on subtle or ambiguous categories. This variation reflects both the limitations of FER2013 and the biological reality that some emotions are inherently harder to recognise.

\subsection{Picking a dataset}
I choose the FER2013 dataset because it offers a practical and accessible starting point in developing an emotion detection model. Because this dataset is widely used in academic projects, the data is already pre-labeled, easy to load and works well with popular deep-learning libraries, which made the experimentation much faster. Even though the dataset has its limitations (low resolution images and some class imbalance), it still profides a solid base for understanding how facial emotion recognition works. Being the most convenient and beginner-friendly option, I considered that training a model on this dataset, I can obtain a good proof of concept and maybe later on move forward on a much bigger and demographic diverse dataset (eg., AffectNet).

\subsubsection{Altering the dataset}
After analysing the confusion matrices and F1-scores of models A and B (7 classes, 25 epochs and 25 epochs), I saw that several classes showed low separability and high misclassification rates ("False Positives"). Emotions such as \emph{fear}, \emph{sad} and \emph{disgust} showed strong overlap, due to the similar features (frowning eyebrows, open mouth, wide open eyes), while \emph{neutral} class overlapped with both positive and negative classes. These patterns show a clear limitation of the FER2013 dataset, where subtle emotions are difficult to classify. \\
Even after increasing the training time from 25 epochs to 25 epochs, the model still struggled in this area. After analysing the dataset and the project itself, it came to my mind that I did not need to know exactly how the end user felt, but just to now if he is in a bad or good mood. That's where I decided to narrow the classes and make them broader. Thus the emotions were grouped into 3 broader categories:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Positive} class that contained the \emph{happy}, \emph{surprise} classes.
\item \textbf{Negative} class that containted the \emph{angry}, \emph{sad}, \emph{fear}, \emph{disgust} classes.
\item \textbf{Neutral} class that contained the \emph{neutral} class.
\end{itemize}

\section{Desktop Application: Study Buddy}

\subsection{Application Overview}
To demonstrate the practical applicability of the emotion detection model (Model C), I developed a lightweight desktop application. The goal of the application is to support healthy studying habits by combining time management with context-aware mood feedback. The application runs in the background as a system tray tool and assists the user during study sessions by suggesting breaks or providing motivational feedback based on how the user is doing.
\subsection{Application Design}
The application is designed as a system tray application using PyQt6. Instead of a traditional windiw-based interface, I opted for a tray application to minimize distractions and and reduce fatigue when switching tabs on your personal computer. If the user disables the camera, the application does now run interference anymore and acts only as a timer, giving alerts only when the study session is about to end. \\
Through the tray menu, the user can:
\begin{itemize}
\item Start, pause, resume or stop the study session.
\item Select predefined session durations (25, 45, 60, 90, 120 minutes).
\item Enter a custom session duration.
\item Enable or disable the camera.
\item Exit the application.
\end{itemize}
The session status and the remaining time are displayed dynamically in the tray tooltip and menu, giving users quick access to checking their progress without interrupting their workflow.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{images/app_preview.png}
    \caption{Preview of the application menu from the system tray}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Study Session and Time Management}
At the core, the applicaiton is a study session controller that tracks the time that you study. The application sens time-based notification, letting the user know the study session is comming to an end (60, 30, 10 minutes remaining). This timer functionality suports the application's primary purpose: helping the user structure their study time and avoid burnout and fatigue.

\subsection{Mood Detection Pipeline}
When the camera is enabled, the application is able to scan your face periodically and perform short emotion scans. These scans occur once per minute and run in the background thread to ensure the user interface remains responsive.

The emotion detection pipeline consists of the following steps:
\begin{itemize}
\item Webcam input: The frames are captured using OpenCV
\item Face detection: A Haar cascade classifier is used to locate a face in each frame
\item Preprocessing: The images captured from step one are resized to 224x224 pixels and normalized
\item Model Interference: The preprocessed image is passed to the trained MobileNet model (Model C)
\item Prediction Stabilisation: Multiple predictions are collected during a short scan and combined using a majority-vote strategy to reduce single-frame missclassifications.
\end{itemize}
 
\subsection{Mood-Based Decision Logic}
The mood-based decision logic is responsible for deciding when the application should interact with the user. Instead of responding immediately to every emotion detected by the camera, this logic evaluates the moods collected from the camera over time and determines whether the application should prompt a notification or not. This apporach is the most realistic one because facial expressions can change quickly and single detections will not represent how the user is feeling during a longer study session.\\
Each time the application performs a scan, the camera stays open approximately 4 second, capturing multiple frames to produce a final mood classification. This classification can be neutral, positive or negative and represents the dominant facial expression during that scan. After a mood is produced, it is stored together with a timestamp at which it was detected. The results are kept in the RAM, representing the user's most recent emotional state. Only results from a predefined period are stored, while the older entries are automatically removed. This ensures that decisions are always based on current information and help keep the application as lightweight as possible.\\
Once a new mood had been added to the history, the decision logic analyses the stored data by counting how often each mood occured within the period. The system looks for patterns that indicate a consistent emotional state rather than reacting to a single negative result. For example, if an user is in a positive state until the scan, but then his facial expression changes suddenly during the scan and gets classified as a negative mood, that is considered a misclassification and the app should not tell the user to take a break. This way the logic looks for repeated negative detections within a period to notify the user to take a break.\\
A break notification is triggered only when two conditions are met:
\begin{itemize}
\item The most recent mood detection must be negative, ensuring that the user is experiencing difficulty.
\item The number of negative mood detections within the period must reached the predefined threshold (eg., 5 negative detections in a row).
\end{itemize} 
With these 2 conditions combined, we avoid spamming the user with notifications by waiting until there is sufficient evidence that a break may be needed.\\
To prevent the application from interrupting the user too frequently, the decision logic has a cooldown mechanism. After a notification is shown, the system temporarily disables further notifications of the same type. During this cooldown period, new mood results are still recorded, but no additional messages are displayed. This design choice helps maintain a supportive and non-intrusive user experience.\\

When the recent mood history consists mainly of neutral or positive detections and no break suggestion has been triggered recently, the system may display a motivational message instead. These messages are intended to encourage the user to continue their study session and provide positive reinforcement without implying that the user is struggling.
\subsection{Privacy-Aware Design}
All image processing and emotion interference occur locally on the user's device. No images, video frames or personal information that may lead to identifying an individual are stored or transmitted. The webcam feature if fully opt-in and users can disable mood detection at any time via the tray menu.\\
The design alignes with GDPR and EU AI Act principles such as data minimisation and purpose limitation.
\subsection{Limitations}
While the application successfully demonstrates the integration of emotion recognition into a practical system, several limitations remain. The face detection relies on a Haar cascade, which may struggle under poor lighting conditions or with non-frontal faces. Additionally, the emotion model is trained on FER2013, which limits its robustness across diverse demographics. Finally, the application provides supportive feedback only and is not suitable for high-stakes or clinical use.

That is when I stopped thinking as a "Software Engineer", but more like a "Data Scientist". I realized that trying to make the model accurately detect the 7 classes was just a losing battle. I found myself again taking a step back and returning to the initial project ideea. The point of my model was to detect if the person using my application was not feeling well and needed a break from studying, not to detect how he was feeling. I managed to get a precision of 89\% on "Positive" classes, 71\% on "Neutral" classes and 81\% on "Negative". After going throught the "Neutral" photos to see why I got a relatively low Recall (57\%) compared to the other classes, I saw that there are some cases where the images were really similar. So it was not a model problem, it was a dataset problem.
